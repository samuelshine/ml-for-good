Who Gets the Call?

Predicting 30-day hospital readmission for diabetic patients

The problem

1 in 11diabetic inpatients readmitted within 30 days
$15,000cost of one avoidable readmission
$75cost of one nurse follow-up call

The gap is not knowing that calls work. It is knowing who to call.

  • Beneficiaries: diabetic inpatients, discharge nurses, safety-net hospitals
  • Target: P(readmission < 30 days), evaluated at discharge
  • Impact measured as precision@10% and lift@10% — not accuracy, which is meaningless at a 9% base rate

Cleaning was the hard part

  • 101,766 rows are only 71,518 patients — one appears 40 times
  • Kept each patient's first encounter, as Strack et al. did
  • Dropped 2,423 encounters ending in death or hospice — they cannot be readmitted
  • Positive rate fell 11.16% → 8.98%: repeat encounters skew positive, so encounter-level modelling flatters itself

What the data says

  • Readmission rises monotonically with prior admissions: 8.1% → 29.3%
  • We reproduce the source paper's HbA1c finding — a credibility check on our cleaning

Stacking uses 5-fold internal CV, so base learners only ever produce out-of-fold predictions — that is what prevents meta-leakage.

Does the ensemble beat the baseline?

Yes — significantly, but modestly.

+0.0107 ROC-AUC, bootstrap 95% CI [+0.0043, +0.0171] over 2,000 resamples. The interval excludes zero.

An AUC near 0.66 matches the published ceiling on this dataset. The limit is the data: housing, food security and caregiver support drive readmission and none are in a discharge record. Reporting 0.95 here would mean a leak, not a breakthrough.

The threshold is a rationing decision

  • A missed readmission costs ~200× a call, so unconstrained optimisation says "call everyone" — useless for a real clinic
  • Capacity, not cost, is the binding constraint
  • At 10% capacity: 21.9% precision vs 9.0% base rate = 2.44× more at-risk patients reached

Why the model says what it says

  • Prior inpatient visits dominate, matching the EDA and mutual information independently
  • Actionable levers: HbA1c testing, medication reconciliation, discharge destination
  • Descriptive only: age, race, prior visit counts

Equal need, unequal service.

African American patients have a readmission rate of 9.23% versus 9.07% for Caucasian patients — yet they are flagged for follow-up 8.0% of the time versus 10.2%, with recall 19.9% versus 25.0%.

Deeper still: only same-network readmissions are recorded, so mobile and uninsured patients are systematically labelled negative. No metric above can detect this — they are all computed against the same biased labels.

What this model cannot do

  • Near-random (AUC 0.566) on repeat high-utilisers vs 0.687 on first admissions — weakest where need is greatest
  • Fails the four-fifths rule on race, payer and age → not deployment-ready
  • 1999–2008 US data; not transferable to Indian clinics without prospective revalidation
  • Triage aid only — never care denial, insurance pricing, or discharge decisions
  • De-identified at source, no PII added, all demo records synthetic

The fairness audit was not a box to tick. It produced a result that blocks release — which is what an audit is for.
